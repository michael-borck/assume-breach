# Introduction: Assume Breach 

A security control can fail. A supplier account can be misused, a patch can
arrive late, or a colleague can approve an unexpected login request. A defender
plans for those possibilities while still trying to prevent them. Assume
breach means designing detection, containment and recovery into the system.

## Who this book is for

This is a first course for students, people moving into security, and curious
professionals. It assumes basic familiarity with a browser and files, but no
security background or programming. The core reading and exercises require
no software installation, paid service, AI account or group.

By the end, you should be able to take a small system description, identify
important assets and plausible threats, choose layered controls within a
budget, specify tests of those controls, and defend a response/recovery
decision with its uncertainties. This is introductory judgement, not
authorisation to test other people's systems or a qualification to run a
production incident alone.

## How the chapters build

Read in order on your first pass. Each part contributes to a short
defender's dossier, using the fictional Harbour Community Centre in
The Defender's Dossier.

| Part | What you should be able to do afterwards |
|---|---|
| I: Foundations | Identify assets, threats, permissions and appropriate protection mechanisms |
| II: Managing Risk | Justify a control choice and check a recovery plan against stated targets |
| III: Systems and Networks | Trace a trust boundary and test permitted and forbidden actions |
| IV: People and Threats | Separate evidence from inference and design a usable verification process |
| V: Putting It Together | Defend a complete plan for a changed case and identify what is not ready |

Every chapter has questions to consider and a short applied task. Try the
task before reading the worked dossier. The final chapter gives an
independent variant and success criteria. Definitions alone are not evidence
that you can use them.

The network primer is in chapter 7. NTLM, cryptographic attack models and
named tools provide optional depth; you do not need to configure them to
complete the dossier. The practice appendix
explains boundaries for optional hands-on work. It does not claim that a
generic VM or container setting creates a safe lab.

Several chapters link Incident Zero, an optional print-and-play group
game. It offers a different experience of making decisions under pressure.
The book's solo exercises provide a complete route if you never play.

## Optional AI conversations

An assistant can explain a term, challenge a proposed control or quiz you.
Try your own answer first, then ask it to identify an assumption you missed.
Keep the evidence trail: a fluent explanation or invented incident is not
a verified source. Three chapters include longer conversation prompts;
other chapters use calculation, annotation and evidence checks.

Use fictional inputs from this book. Do not paste real phishing emails,
credentials, session tokens, personal information, internal logs or production
firewall rules into a public assistant. If you adapt an example, remove
sensitive details and follow your organisation's approved data-handling rules.
A generated configuration is a draft to test in an authorised environment,
never a ready-to-deploy answer.

For more on this method, see
Conversation, Not Delegation.
No companion book is required here.

# Introduction to Information Security

> What are we protecting, from whom, and how would we even know if we'd succeeded?

Begin with a system you can describe, then ask what a failure would harm.
This chapter gives names to the protection goals and introduces the learning
map used throughout the book. The first paper task starts your dossier.

## What this chapter covers

By the end you should be able to:

- Define information security and explain the CIA triad: confidentiality, integrity,
  availability.
- Explain when those three can conflict, and what "balancing CIA" means in practice.
- Say what a security control is, and what defence in depth buys you.
- Walk through the phases of an attack, and explain why a defender studies them.
- Distinguish ethical security work from malicious hacking, and see where the line sits.

## Security is a property, not a product

You cannot buy security in a box. There is no single product, no switch, no certificate that
makes a system "secure." Security is not a thing a system has. It is a property of how the
whole system behaves, including the people using it. A bank vault with the door propped open is
not secure no matter how thick the steel. A perfectly configured server whose administrator
uses "password1" is not secure either. Security depends on how those choices interact. A weakness may expose one asset
while other controls limit the damage.

That is why this book keeps returning to a defender's habit of mind rather than a checklist.
Checklists go stale. The habit of asking "what am I protecting, from whom, and how would I know
if it failed?" does not.

## The CIA triad

Almost everything in information security can be traced back to protecting one of three
properties of information. Together they are called the CIA triad (no relation to the
agency).

Confidentiality is keeping information from those who should not see it. When you think of
"security," this is usually what comes to mind: secrets stay secret. Encryption, access
controls, and passwords are all, at heart, confidentiality mechanisms. A confidentiality
failure is a leak: data ending up in the wrong hands.

Integrity is keeping information correct and un-tampered. It is less obvious than
confidentiality but often matters more. If an attacker cannot read your bank balance but can
change it, you have a worse problem than a leak. Integrity mechanisms let you detect or
prevent unauthorised change. A hospital needs to trust that a patient's recorded blood type is
the one a doctor entered, not one an attacker altered.

Availability is keeping information and systems there when they are needed. A system nobody
can reach is no more useful than one that was deleted. Availability is the property attacked by
a denial-of-service, threatened by a single point of failure, and protected by backups,
redundancy, and capacity planning. It is the property people forget until the day the system is
down and the business stops.

### Balancing CIA

The CIA properties can conflict, but they do not inevitably work against
each other. A tested backup can improve both integrity and availability.
Well-managed access can protect confidentiality while helping authorised
users find the right information.

Trade-offs arise in particular designs. Extra approval steps may delay
urgent access. More replicas improve availability but create more copies to
protect. Preventing every change would preserve an old record while making
a booking system unusable. Ask which harm matters, who is affected, and
what evidence shows the control meets the need. A hospital's emergency-access
arrangement can be different from a newspaper's embargo process without
either abandoning security.

A fourth idea often sits alongside the triad: non-repudiation, the property that someone
cannot credibly deny having done something. An audit trail and a digital signature can support a claim about an action.
They still depend on trustworthy identity binding, key control and records.
An account identifier alone does not prove which human acted or settle a dispute.

## Controls and defence in depth

A security control is anything you put between an asset and a threat to reduce risk: a
password, a firewall, a locked door, a policy, a training session, a backup. Controls come in
familiar flavours: some prevent (a lock stops entry), some detect (an alarm notices
it), and some correct (a backup restores what was lost). A good defence uses all three,
because prevention eventually fails.

This is the idea the book is named for. Defence in depth means never trusting a single
control to be enough. You assume each layer will fail, and you put another behind it. A
castle did not rely on the outer wall alone. It had a moat, then a wall, then a gate, then an
inner keep, so that breaching one defence still left the attacker facing the next. A modern
system is the same: a firewall and patched software and least-privilege accounts and
monitoring and backups. Layers reduce dependence on any one control. Shared credentials, a common
administrator or a shared power supply can still defeat several layers together.

This is the practical face of assume breach. If you assume the attacker will get past your
first control, you stop asking "is this wall strong enough?" and start asking "when this fails,
what catches it?" That question builds better systems than the search for one perfect wall ever
could.

## The phases of an attack

To defend against attackers, you have to understand how they work. You are not learning this to
imitate them. You study each step to see what it needs, so you can take that away. Most
intrusions move through recognisable phases:

1. Reconnaissance. The attacker gathers information about the target from the outside:
   who works there, what systems they run, what's exposed. Quiet, often legal, and easy to
   overlook.
2. Scanning. They probe the target directly for live systems, open ports, and running
   services, mapping the attack surface. In this book you can reason from supplied
   observations without probing any real system.
3. Gaining access. They exploit a weakness (a flaw, a weak password, a tricked user) to
   get a foothold.
4. Maintaining access. They entrench, so a reboot or a changed password doesn't lock them
   out.
5. Clearing tracks. They cover their trail, deleting logs and hiding activity to delay
   discovery.

Read that list as a defender and each phase becomes an opportunity. Reconnaissance can be
made harder by exposing less. Scanning can be detected. Access can be denied with patching and
strong authentication. Persistence and track-clearing can be spotted by monitoring that the
attacker cannot fully erase. Understanding the attacker's process is where a defender's leverage
comes from.

## Ethical hacking, and the line

Much of what you'll learn in this book is, in mechanism, identical to what an attacker does:
scanning systems, cracking passwords, capturing traffic. The difference is permission and
purpose. An ethical practitioner acts with the owner's authorisation, within an agreed scope,
to find and fix weaknesses before a real attacker exploits them. Unapproved testing may be unlawful and can harm others. Treat explicit
permission and agreed scope as prerequisites, not optional courtesy.

This is where the old "hats" come from. A white hat works with authorisation, to find and
fix. A black hat breaks in for their own gain or to cause harm. A grey hat acts without
permission while believing the intent is good. Good intentions do not move the line. What
separates the three is authorisation.

That line is sharp and it matters. Practise only in an environment you own or are explicitly
authorised to use. The skills are dual-use. Your professionalism is what makes them defensive.

## Try it yourself

Begin with the fictional, paper-based Harbour case.
Optional technical practice requires an explicitly authorised environment with
checked boundaries. The practice appendix
explains why a VM, NAT connection or container does not itself establish isolation.

## A lifecycle to revisit

Use seven activities as a learning map: design, harden, detect, respond,
recover, investigate, and audit/learn. People and decisions run through all
seven. Activities overlap: evidence collection can begin during response,
and a recovery test can reveal a design flaw before any breach occurs.
This is the book's teaching map, not a mandated sequence.
NIST's incident-response guidance
places incident work within continuing risk management.

## Where this connects

The CIA triad and defence in depth are the lenses for the whole book. Almost every
later control exists to protect one leg of the triad or to add one more layer. The next chapter
begins the technical detail, looking at where software goes wrong and the malware that walks
through the gaps.

## Questions to consider

1. Give a real situation where confidentiality matters most, one where integrity
   matters most, and one where availability matters most. Use three different examples.
2. "More security is always better." Describe one case where tightening security actually made
   things worse. What was traded away?
3. Why would a defender bother learning the phases of an attack? What specifically do you gain
   by understanding how an attacker thinks?

## Map an asset

Read Harbour's facts. In 10 minutes, choose one asset and write a four-column
row: harm, plausible threat, control, and evidence that the control works.
Then name one residual failure. A good row connects a specific harm to a test;
“use encryption” without saying what it protects is not enough.

# Software Security & Malware

> A software flaw can give an attacker a foothold. What happens next depends on the access and controls around it.

Software vulnerabilities, stolen credentials and deceptive messages can each
give an attacker a foothold. This chapter concentrates on software flaws and
malware, then uses a historical breach to show how several failures can connect.
Chapter 10 returns to the criminal market and the evidence left behind.

## What this chapter covers

By the end you should be able to:

- Distinguish design flaws from implementation flaws, and explain attack surface.
- Explain what CVE and CWE are, and how vulnerability disclosure works
  (responsible disclosure, zero-day).
- Classify the main families of malware: viruses, worms, spyware, adware, trojans, RATs,
  ransomware, rootkits, and botnets.
- Explain how a trojan and a botnet actually work, and how we detect and prevent them.
- Trace how a real breach chains several of these together.

## Where flaws come from

Software fails in two ways, and the distinction matters because they are fixed differently.

A design flaw is a mistake in the plan. The software does what it was designed to do, and
the design itself is insecure. A system that emails you your own password in plain text has no
bug. It works as intended, and the intention is the problem. Design flaws are expensive because
fixing them means changing the plan, often after everything has been built on top of it.

An implementation flaw is a mistake in the building. The design was sound, but somewhere in
the code a programmer made an error: mishandled a length, trusted an input they shouldn't have,
forgot a check. Implementation flaws are one reason software needs patching. A patch may also
change an insecure design; the categories help us reason but can overlap.

Every flaw is only a threat if an attacker can reach it. This brings us to attack surface:
the sum of all the points where an attacker could try to get in. Every open port, every input
field, every feature, every piece of installed software adds to it. A defender's instinct,
seen already in the last chapter, is to reduce attack surface: turn off what you don't use,
close what you don't need, and you shrink the number of doors an attacker can try. Attack
surface is not fixed. It grows every time you add a feature, install software, or connect a new
system, which is why security is never "done."

## Naming the weaknesses: CVE and CWE

When there are millions of flaws across the world's software, you need a way to talk about them
precisely. Two catalogues do this. A CVE (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures) is a unique
identifier for a vulnerability record, which can identify affected products and versions.
A shared flaw may affect more than one product; the identifier gives people
a common reference. A CWE (Common Weakness
Enumeration) is one level up: a category of type of flaw ("improper input validation,"
"use after free") that recurs across many products. CVEs are the individual cases; CWEs are the
patterns behind them. The CVE programme
and CWE catalogue describe their different purposes.

### Disclosure and the zero-day

When a researcher finds a flaw, coordinated vulnerability disclosure aims
to get useful information to affected parties while reducing harm. A researcher
reports through an authorised channel and coordinates with the vendor or a
relevant response team. Publication timing must account for active exploitation,
user protection and unresponsive vendors; it is not always “wait until patched”.
CERT/CC's disclosure guide
explains the roles and trade-offs.

A zero-day vulnerability is commonly discussed as a flaw for which
defenders do not yet have an available fix, often before public disclosure.
Distinguish the vulnerability from a zero-day exploit or attack that uses it.
Terminology varies, so describe whether the vendor knew, whether a patch
existed and whether exploitation was observed. Layered controls matter when
patching cannot yet remove the exposure.

## Malware: a field guide

Malware (malicious software) is the general term for any program written to do harm. The
families are worth knowing because they spread and survive differently, and each difference is
a place to defend.

### Viruses and worms

A virus attaches itself to a legitimate file or program and spreads when that host is run
and shared. It needs a carrier and, usually, a human to move it. A macro virus hides in
the scripting built into documents (a spreadsheet or word-processor file), which is why "enable
macros?" is a question worth treating with suspicion.

A worm is the more dangerous cousin: it spreads on its own, across the network, without
needing a host file or a human to carry it. The 1988 Morris Worm, one of the first, showed
the pattern that still holds: self-propagating code can move faster than any human response.
For a defender, a worm's fuel is connectivity and unpatched systems, so segmentation and
patching are what starve it.

### Spyware, adware, and the willing click

Not all malware exploits a software flaw. Spyware collects information (keystrokes,
browsing, credentials) and reports it back. Adware forces unwanted advertising and often
tracks you to do it. A great deal of malware needs no vulnerability at all: it needs the user
to agree, to click "install," to run the attachment, to grant the permission. This is the
theme that returns in the human-factors chapter: the most patched system in the world can still
be undone by one willing click.

## Trojans, RATs, and botnets

A trojan horse is malware disguised as something desirable: a game, a cracked application,
a useful tool. The user runs it willingly, and while it may even do what it claims, it also does
something else. A Remote Access Trojan (RAT) is a nasty variant: it hands the attacker
remote control of the machine (files, webcam, keystrokes, the lot) as though they were sitting
at it.

Scale this up and you get a botnet: a network of many compromised machines ("bots") under
one attacker's control, taking orders from a command-and-control server. A botnet's owner can
rent it out to send spam, mine cryptocurrency, or overwhelm a target with a distributed
denial-of-service (DDoS) attack. The infamous Zeus family, for example, turned huge numbers of
ordinary PCs into a credential-stealing network. The defender's insight is that a botnet's value
is aggregate. No single infected machine matters much, which is why individual users
underestimate the cost of being compromised.

Ransomware can encrypt data and disrupt services; attackers may also steal
data and threaten to publish it. Tested, protected backups support recovery
but cannot undo disclosure. Paying does not guarantee either restoration or
deletion of stolen copies. Response therefore includes containment, assessment
of exposed data and recovery, not just obtaining a decryption key.
CISA's ransomware guide
covers these connected risks.

## Rootkits: hiding in the floor

A rootkit is malware that subverts a low level of the system (the operating system, or
below it) so that it can hide everything above. By compromising the layer the rest of the
system trusts to report the truth, a rootkit can make its own files, processes, and network
connections invisible to the tools you'd normally use to find them. This is why compromised
machines are often rebuilt from scratch rather than "cleaned": once you can't trust the floor
you're standing on, you can't trust anything it tells you.

## Defending against malware

No single control stops all of this. That is why the book argues for layered defence. Signature-based detection recognises known patterns; other endpoint techniques
look at behaviour or suspicious activity. Neither guarantees detection of an
unfamiliar attack, so endpoint protection must be one layer among many. Patching closes the flaws worms and exploits need. Least privilege
stops a compromised program reaching everything. Controls limit what software is allowed to run.
User education targets the willing click. Monitoring catches the intrusion that gets through
anyway. "We have antivirus" is not a security strategy. It is one thin layer, and a defender
who relies on it alone has assumed the breach won't happen.

## How it comes together: a real breach

Real attacks rarely use one flaw. They chain several. The 2013 Target breach is a classic worked
example. Attackers first stole network credentials from a third-party contractor (a
heating-and-cooling vendor with access to Target's systems). They used that foothold to reach the
retail network and installed point-of-sale malware (the "BlackPOS" family) that scraped card data
from checkout terminals. They exfiltrated tens of millions of card records while warning signs
went unheeded. Read it as a defender and every stage is a control that could have caught it:
tighter vendor access, network segmentation, restricted software execution, and someone acting
on the alerts. Several controls offered possible opportunities to interrupt that chain.
Which would have worked depends on implementation and timing. The
2014 Senate staff analysis
discusses possible missed opportunities using the public reporting then
available; it is not proof of a unique counterfactual.

## Play it

Incident Zero, a free, print-and-play security game, has a Hardening module where you
spend a limited budget on layered defences and then a pentester attacks. It's the fastest way
to feel why one control is never enough, and why "we have antivirus" is not a security
strategy. → incidentzero.retroverse.studio

## Where this connects

The introduction laid out the phases of an attack, and this chapter fills in the "gaining
access" phase. Stolen credentials, as in the Target breach, are the entry point the
authentication chapter is about. Botnets and the criminal economy behind malware return in
cybercrime, botnets and forensics.

# Authentication & Access

> A successful login establishes an identity claim. The next question is what that account is allowed to do.

At Harbour, Rae needs to amend a booking but should not be able to export
every participant's details. Checking who signs in and limiting what they
can do are separate controls. This chapter follows both, including how
systems protect password verifiers, recover accounts and end compromised
sessions.

## What this chapter covers

By the end you should be able to:

- Distinguish identification, authentication, and authorisation, and explain
  mutual authentication and ID proofing.
- Explain the three factors of authentication and what multi-factor authentication
  actually adds.
- Describe how a system stores credentials: cryptographic hashing, and why we add
  salt.
- Explain Windows / NTLM authentication at a high level and why it matters in practice.
- Analyse password attacks (dictionary, brute-force, rainbow tables) and the defences
  that beat each one: salting, lockout, length.

## Three words people mix up

These three terms are used loosely in everyday speech and precisely in security, and the
precision matters.

Identification is a claim: you state who you are (a username, an account number, a name).
It is just an assertion, and on its own it proves nothing.

Authentication is the proof of that claim: you demonstrate that you are who you
said. Typing the password that matches the username is authentication. This is the step
attackers most want to defeat, because a successful fake here makes everything downstream trust
them.

Authorisation is what happens after: given that we now believe who you are, what are you
allowed to do? A verified user is not an all-powerful one. Authentication gets you through the
door. Authorisation decides which rooms you may enter. Keeping these separate lets a
system trust who you are while still limiting what you can do. This is the principle of least
privilege in action.

Two refinements are worth naming. Mutual authentication is when both sides prove their
identity. Not only does the server check you, but you (or your software) check the server, so
you're not handing your credentials to an impostor. ID proofing is the harder,
real-world problem of establishing identity in the first place. How does a bank know the person
opening an account is who they claim, before any password exists? Authentication verifies a
claim you set up earlier. Proofing is how that claim was trusted to begin with.

## The three factors

How can you prove who you are? Every method falls into one of three factors:

- Something you know: a password, a PIN, the answer to a question.
- Something you have: a phone, a hardware token, a smart card.
- Something you are: a fingerprint, a face, a voice (biometrics).

Passwords are widely used because they need little special hardware, but
guessing, reuse and phishing make them risky on their own. Long, unique
passwords generated and stored with a password manager reduce guessing and
reuse. Arbitrary complexity rules can encourage predictable substitutions;
rate limiting addresses repeated online guesses. Neither stops a user
submitting a password to an impostor.

### Multi-factor authentication

One important improvement is to require more than one factor:
multi-factor authentication (MFA). The power comes from combining categories: a password
(know) plus a code from your phone (have) means stealing the password alone is no longer enough.
The "have" factor takes many forms: a one-time code, a hardware token or key fob, or a
smart card carrying a chip that proves possession. The "are" factor, biometrics, is
convenient and hard to forget, but carries a permanent trade-off: you can change a breached
password, but you cannot change your fingerprint. MFA is strong, but it is not magic. An attacker may still phish a one-time code
or trick a user into approving a prompt. The type of MFA matters. Passkeys and suitable security keys bind an
authentication response to the legitimate service, resisting a fake login
site in a way a relayed one-time code does not. Their enrolment, device
protection and recovery paths still need care. A biometric often unlocks
an authenticator locally rather than being sent to the website as a password.
NIST's authenticator guidance
distinguishes these mechanisms.

## What a system actually stores

Here is the idea that surprises most people, and it is the hinge of the whole chapter: a
well-built system does not store your password. If it did, anyone who stole the database (or
any administrator) would know every user's secret.

Instead, the system stores a password verifier produced by a purpose-built
password-hashing scheme. At login it derives a verifier from the supplied
password and stored parameters, then compares the result. A cryptographic
hash is designed to resist inversion, but attackers can still guess inputs.

The scheme must make each guess expensive through a suitable work factor,
and often memory cost. A fast general-purpose hash alone is not appropriate
password storage. Libraries implementing schemes such as Argon2id handle
these details; do not invent a scheme from hash primitives.
OWASP's password-storage guidance
explains the choice and parameters.

### Windows and NTLM

Windows' legacy NTLM authentication is useful optional background.
It uses password-derived credential material in a challenge/response scheme.
Stolen NTLM hash material may enable pass-the-hash authentication without
recovering the plaintext password. Treat it as a credential, not as harmless
scrambled data. It is not a model for designing a new password store.
Microsoft's NTLM overview
describes the protocol, and
NIST's threat guidance
includes reuse of stolen authentication material.

## Attacking passwords

If attackers can get the hashes but can't reverse them, how do they recover passwords? They
don't reverse the hash. They guess, hash each guess, and look for a match.

A dictionary attack works through a list of likely passwords (common words, previously
leaked passwords, predictable patterns) because real people rarely choose randomly. A
brute-force attack tries every possible combination. For a finite candidate space it can exhaust every candidate, but the work may
be infeasible. Length helps when it adds unpredictability; a long, well-known
phrase can still be guessed early. The
accelerator is a rainbow table: a giant precomputed lookup of hashes for enormous
numbers of candidate passwords, so the attacker trades storage for speed and skips the
recomputation.

### The fix: salt

Rainbow tables have one fatal weakness, and the fix is elegant. Before hashing, the
system adds a unique random value, a salt, to each password, and stores the salt alongside
the hash. Now two users with the same password have different hashes, and the
attacker's precomputed rainbow table, built without your specific salt, is worthless. Salting
doesn't make any single password harder to guess. It prevents useful reuse of the same precomputed table across different salts.
The attacker must work separately for each salt. The password-hashing scheme's
cost settings, not the salt alone, make each guess expensive.

Distinguish online guessing against a login service from offline
guessing against stolen verifiers. Login rate limits and lockouts do not
slow an attacker working on their own copy. Long unique passwords, proper
verifier storage and protection of the database address that different threat.

## The maths is rarely the weakest link

Everything above assumes the attacker has to defeat authentication. Often they go
around it. A keylogger records the password as you type it, before any hashing happens.
Social engineering just asks. A convincing email or phone call, and the user hands the
secret over. Sniffing captures credentials sent in the clear across a network (which returns
in the network-security chapter). And stealing the password file itself gives the attacker
the hashes to work on at leisure. The recurring lesson of this book applies here: the
cryptography is rarely what fails. The people and the plumbing around it are.

## A word on single sign-on

One more idea you'll meet: single sign-on (SSO) lets you authenticate once and gain access to
many systems, and reduced sign-on is a lighter version of the same convenience. Users love it,
and it improves security in one way: fewer passwords means less reuse and fewer
sticky notes. But it concentrates risk: a compromised identity provider or session may expose several services.
SSO must still enforce per-service permissions, restricted administration
and revocation; it need not give every account access to everything. Protect the identity provider and recovery process with appropriate MFA.

## Permissions and recovery are part of the design

A receptionist may need to amend one booking without being allowed to export
every participant's details. Enforce that rule on the server for each action,
not just by hiding a button. Review access when duties change or people leave.

Recovery is another entry path. A help desk that bypasses all checks when a
caller sounds urgent can undermine strong login controls. Plan an independent
identity check, recovery credentials where appropriate, and revocation of
affected sessions. Changing a password alone does not prove that every
existing session has ended.

## Where this connects

The hashing this chapter relies on is the subject of the next chapter, cryptography.
Sniffing for credentials reappears in network security, when we capture packets on the
wire. And stolen credentials, as in the Target breach, are how many intrusions begin, a thread
running back to software security and malware.

# Cryptography

> A sound cipher should remain secure when its design is public and only the key is secret.

Cryptography can protect information sent through an untrusted network,
but different operations solve different problems. Encrypting a booking,
hashing an export and signing a message do not provide interchangeable
assurances. You do not need to derive the algorithms here; you need to
identify the protection, its assumptions and the systems it depends on.

## What this chapter covers

By the end you should be able to:

- Explain the difference between an algorithm and a key, and state Kerckhoff's
  principle.
- Distinguish symmetric (one-key) from asymmetric (two-key) cryptography and say what
  each is good for.
- Explain hashing vs encryption, and where digital signatures fit.
- Reason about attacks: brute-forcing small key spaces, known-plaintext and chosen-plaintext
  attacks.

## The algorithm and the key

Every cipher has two parts, and telling them apart is the foundation of everything else. The
algorithm is the method: the fixed procedure for scrambling and unscrambling. The key
is the secret input that steers it: the particular setting that turns readable plaintext into
scrambled ciphertext and back. The same algorithm with a different key produces different
output. The set of all possible keys is the key space, and its size is one measure
of a cipher's strength: the more keys an attacker would have to try, the better.

Here is where beginners' intuition usually goes wrong. It feels safer to keep the algorithm
secret too. Surely hiding the method helps? Kerckhoff's principle says the opposite, and it
is one of the most important ideas in the whole field:

> A cryptosystem should be secure even if everything about it, except the key, is public
> knowledge.

In other words, the security must rest entirely on the secrecy of the key, never on the
secrecy of the algorithm. This sounds counter-intuitive until you see why it's a strength. Keeping a design secret can limit independent review without demonstrating
that the design is sound. If secrecy of the algorithm is the only protection, disclosure removes it. A public algorithm,
published and attacked by the world's cryptographers for years, has earned its trust the hard
way. This is why every cipher you should trust (the ones protecting your web traffic, your
messages, your disk) has an openly published algorithm and keeps only the key secret. "Secret
sauce" cryptography is a warning sign.

## Symmetric cryptography: one shared key

The oldest and simplest arrangement is symmetric (or one-key) cryptography: the same key
both encrypts and decrypts. If you and I share a secret key, I encrypt with it, you decrypt with
it, and anyone without it sees only noise. Symmetric ciphers are fast and efficient, which is why
they do the heavy lifting of bulk encryption: encrypting a whole disk, a database, or a stream
of network traffic.

Symmetric cryptography has one stubborn problem, and it's not the maths: key distribution.
Before we can communicate securely, we both need the same secret key. But how do I get it to
you securely in the first place? If we could already send secrets safely, we wouldn't need the
encryption. For two people who have never met, sharing a key over an untrusted network is
the problem the whole system was meant to solve. For centuries this was cryptography's
central limitation.

## Asymmetric cryptography: a key you can publish

Asymmetric cryptography uses a mathematically related public/private
key pair. The public key can be shared; the private key must be protected.
Different schemes perform different jobs. Do not assume every pair can
encrypt, decrypt and sign interchangeably.

With a public-key encryption scheme, a sender encrypts to the recipient's
public key and the recipient decrypts using the private key. But the sender
must establish that this really is the recipient's key. Publishing a key
does not by itself solve the problem of authenticating it.

A digital signature is a separate operation: a signing algorithm uses a
private key, and a verification algorithm uses the corresponding public key.
Signing is not generally “encrypting with the private key”. Verification
checks the signature against the message. A valid signature supports a claim
about the signing key, subject to trustworthy key identity and control;
it does not by itself prove a human's intent.
NIST's Digital Signature Standard
describes these distinct signing and verification operations.

Real systems often combine public-key techniques with fast symmetric
protection. In a certificate-authenticated TLS connection, the browser checks
the server's certificate and name, the parties establish traffic keys, and
symmetric authenticated encryption protects the traffic. Modern key agreement
can derive a shared secret rather than sending a symmetric key encrypted
with the server's public key.
TLS 1.3 separates these steps.
If certificate validation fails, do not bypass the warning just to continue.

## Hashing: one-way, and not encryption

A hash function is often grouped with cryptography but does something different, and the
difference matters. Encryption is reversible. With the right key, decryption recovers the original. A cryptographic hash is deliberately one-way: it turns any input into a
fixed-length fingerprint that cannot be reversed to recover the input. You met this in the last
chapter, where systems store the hash of a password rather than the password itself.

Encryption aims to protect confidentiality. A cryptographic hash gives a
compact comparison value designed to make it infeasible to find a different
input with the same digest. Collisions are mathematically possible; “every
change must produce a different hash” is not the guarantee.

To check a downloaded file, compare its digest with an expected value obtained
through a trusted channel. If an attacker replaces both the file and its
advertised digest, comparison alone proves nothing about authenticity.
A keyed message authentication code or a signature can add authentication
under the relevant key assumptions.

Encryption alone also need not detect tampering. Authenticated encryption
combines confidentiality with an integrity check, rejecting altered protected
data. Use a maintained protocol or library rather than assembling your own
combination. Key theft, a compromised endpoint or an authorised but malicious
application session can still expose the plaintext.

## Attacks, and the limits of weak crypto

Understanding how ciphers fail sharpens the intuition. The simplest failure is a key space
too small to matter. The classic Caesar cipher shifts each letter by a fixed amount. But there
are only twenty-five possible shifts, so an attacker tries them all in seconds. A
monoalphabetic substitution cipher, where each letter maps to another, has an enormous key
space yet still falls easily, because it leaks the structure of the language: letter
frequencies survive the substitution, and 'e' is still the commonest letter. A big key space
is necessary but not sufficient. A cipher can be broken by the patterns it fails to hide.

Cryptographers also reason about what an attacker gets to see. In a known-plaintext attack,
the attacker has some plaintext and its matching ciphertext and uses the pair to attack the
key. In the stronger chosen-plaintext attack, they can feed in plaintext of their choosing
and study the output. A cipher worth trusting must withstand even an adversary with those
advantages. That is why real ciphers are published and battered by experts rather than kept
secret and hoped over.

Well-chosen modern cryptography can make direct cryptanalytic attacks
impractical. Attackers may instead exploit the surrounding system.
They steal the key from a poorly protected file, trick a user into revealing it, exploit a bug
in the implementation of a sound algorithm, or take the data before it's encrypted or after
it's decrypted. Even ransomware, which weaponises encryption against you, succeeds through the
plumbing (how it gets in and what it can reach) rather than by breaking the cipher. The defender must examine both the cryptographic choice and its operating
conditions; neither should be assumed correct merely because encryption is present.

## Where this connects

Password verification uses the hashing ideas introduced in authentication. The
encrypted tunnel a VPN builds rests on the symmetric, asymmetric, and key-exchange ideas here,
as VPN and firewalls will show. And the secure connection behind every reputable website is
the hybrid scheme described above, which returns in web security and data protection.

1. Why is it a good thing that strong ciphers publish their algorithms openly?
2. Symmetric crypto is faster; asymmetric solves key distribution. Sketch how a real system
   might use both.
3. What's the practical difference between hashing a message and encrypting it? When
   would you want each?

# Risk Management

> You can't protect everything equally, so you don't. Risk management is how a defender decides where the money and effort go.

Everything before this chapter was about how to defend: controls, cryptography, layered
defence. This chapter is about where to spend the effort. It is the moment security stops
being a technical subject and becomes a business one. No organisation has unlimited money, time,
or patience, so a defender who tries to protect everything equally protects nothing well. Risk
management is the discipline of deciding, honestly and defensibly, what to protect, how much to
spend, and what to leave alone. It is also where "assume breach" turns into a budget.

## What this chapter covers

By the end you should be able to:

- Define risk, risk appetite, and explain why appetite varies between organisations.
- Explain cost-benefit analysis and the difference between intrinsic and acquired
  value.
- Calculate single loss expectancy (SLE) and annual loss expectancy (ALE), and use
  them to judge whether a control is worth it.
- Describe the main risk treatments: avoid, mitigate, transfer, accept.

## What risk actually is

In everyday speech "risk" means danger. In security it means something more precise and useful:
risk is the combination of how likely something bad is and how much it would hurt if it
happened. A useful shorthand is risk = likelihood × impact. Neither alone tells you much. A
catastrophic event that almost never happens may be a smaller risk than a minor one that happens
weekly. Treating them as equal, as a fear-driven approach does, wastes effort on the dramatic and
ignores the routine.

Because risk is about likelihood and impact, it is always relative to a particular organisation.
That is where risk appetite comes in: how much risk a given organisation is willing to accept
in pursuit of its goals. A hospital and a social-media start-up face different threats, value
different things, and answer to different regulators, so they will rationally accept different
risks. Risk appetite is a deliberate statement of how much uncertainty an organisation will live
with. It is neither recklessness nor timidity. Two well-run companies facing the same threat can
make opposite, equally defensible decisions. They should be able to explain the constraints behind those choices.

## What are you protecting, and what's it worth?

You cannot judge a risk without knowing the value of what's at stake, and value is subtler than a
price tag. An asset has intrinsic value (what it is worth in itself, like the replacement
cost of a server) and acquired value (the worth it takes on from its role and context). A
customer database might contain a few dollars of storage, but the acquired value (the trust, the
regulatory exposure, the competitive harm if it leaked) can be enormous. Defenders who price only
the intrinsic value under-protect the things that matter most, because the damage from losing
information usually dwarfs the cost of the hardware holding it.

Once you can value what's at stake, you can do cost-benefit analysis: weigh what a control
costs against the loss it prevents. This provides one input to a security decision. It
has an uncomfortable implication people resist: sometimes the right answer is to not buy the
control, because it costs more than the harm it prevents. That judgement is constrained by legal duties, safety and the organisation's
stated limits. A positive financial return is not the only reason to protect people.

## Putting numbers on it: SLE, ARO, ALE

To make cost-benefit concrete, security borrows a simple model from insurance. Three quantities
do the work. Single Loss Expectancy (SLE) is how much one occurrence of the event would cost
you. Annualised Rate of Occurrence (ARO) is how many times a year you expect it to happen,
and it can be a fraction: 0.2 means once every five years. Annual Loss Expectancy (ALE) is
the expected cost per year, `SLE × ARO`.

Here is a worked example. Suppose a breach of your customer database would cost about
\$500,000 to clean up, notify, and recover from. That is the SLE. You judge such a breach
might happen once every five years, so the ARO is 0.2. The annual loss expectancy is:

`ALE = SLE × ARO = \$500,000 × 0.2 = \$100,000 per year.`

That \$100,000 is what this risk "costs" you in expectation each year, and it's the number a
control has to beat.

## Is the control worth it?

Now you can judge a control honestly. Say better monitoring and encryption cost \$40,000 a
year and would cut the breach rate to once every twenty years (ARO 0.05). The new annual loss
expectancy becomes `\$500,000 × 0.05 = \$25,000`. So the control reduces your ALE from
\$100,000 to \$25,000, a benefit of \$75,000 a year, for a cost of \$40,000. It saves you
\$35,000 net, so it is worth buying.

For this financial model, a control has positive expected net benefit when
the reduction in ALE exceeds its annual cost. That is not a complete
acceptance rule for safety, privacy or regulatory obligations. Turn the numbers around and the answer flips. A \$50,000
control that only reduces an ALE from \$30,000 to \$5,000 loses you money, and buying it out of
a vague sense that "more security is better" is the mistake this chapter exists to prevent.

Two honest cautions come with the arithmetic. First, notice that even after the good control,
\$25,000 of annual risk remains: this is residual risk, the risk left over after treatment.
You almost never reduce risk to zero, and pretending otherwise is how organisations get
surprised. Second, these numbers are estimates: SLE and ARO are educated guesses, and some
impacts (a ruined reputation, harm to people, loss of life) resist being priced at all. Treat
quantitative risk analysis as a discipline for thinking clearly. The numbers structure the
judgement; they do not replace it.

## The four things you can do with a risk

Once a risk is identified and sized, you have four options. They are the vocabulary every
security professional shares:

- Avoid. Don't do the risky thing at all. Stop offering the feature, don't collect the
  data, close the exposure. This can remove the particular exposure, but may introduce other risks.
  It is often impractical because the activity is part of the business.
- Mitigate. Reduce the likelihood or the impact with controls. This is most of what the
  rest of this book is about: patch, encrypt, segment, train, monitor. You rarely eliminate the
  risk; you shrink it to an acceptable size.
- Transfer. Share specified financial consequences through insurance or a contract.
  Coverage, exclusions and provider obligations matter. Outsourcing does not
  automatically transfer your responsibility for customers or legal compliance.
- Accept. Decide the risk is small enough, or the fix too costly, to live with it. This is a
  legitimate, deliberate choice, provided it's made consciously and by someone with the
  authority to make it. Record the owner, reasoning, residual exposure and review date. An unexamined
  risk is not the same as deliberate acceptance.

Organisations use resources with different purposes: the ASD Essential Eight
sets out mitigation strategies and maturity expectations; ISO/IEC 27001
specifies an information-security management system; the NIST Cybersecurity
Framework organises cybersecurity outcomes. They are not interchangeable
pass/fail checklists. The NIST CSF
provides a starting point for understanding outcomes and governance. Use the framework to structure evidence and responsibilities, then explain
how the chosen treatment addresses the organisation's actual exposure.

# Incident & Disaster Planning

> The question isn't whether something will go wrong, but whether you're ready when it does.

At Harbour, an unexpected login prompt and a bulk-export event arrive within
minutes of each other. Who can suspend access, preserve the logs, talk to the
service provider and decide what the centre tells participants? This chapter
turns those questions into a response plan. Preparation and recovery tests
belong before the first incident.

## What this chapter covers

By the end you should be able to:

- Distinguish incident response, business continuity, and disaster recovery.
- Explain RTO and RPO and why they drive the plan.
- Describe what a good incident response process looks like (detect → respond → recover →
  learn).
- Explain the role of backups, alternate sites, and testing the plan.

## When prevention runs out

Patching and access controls lower the chance of compromise. Detection,
response and recovery reduce harm when prevention fails. They also help
with mistakes and outages unrelated to an attacker.

Preparation means deciding authority, keeping contact information available,
and testing recovery while there is time to correct a failure. A response
plan should work when the usual administrator is absent and the primary
communication system is unavailable.

## Incident response: the lifecycle

An incident involves an actual or imminent threat to security. A suspicious
event needs triage; staff should not wait for certainty before reporting it.
A response process turns incomplete observations into proportionate action:

- Detect and assess. Record the observation, time and affected service.
  Decide urgency and scope, distinguishing facts from assumptions.
- Respond. Contain damage under assigned authority. Preserve useful
  evidence where feasible while restricting compromised access. Record
  actions that change or destroy evidence and why they were necessary.
- Recover. Address the cause and persistence, restore or rebuild from
  an acceptable state, and validate access and integrity before reconnecting.
  A successful restore is not proof the attacker has been removed.
- Learn. Review controls, decisions and communication. Assign improvements
  and check that they are completed.

These activities overlap. Evidence collection and analysis begin during
response, not only after restoration. Do not indiscriminately wipe a system
before consulting the response lead; equally, do not leave serious harm
continuing solely to obtain perfect evidence.
NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3
places incident response within continuing risk management.

Two things make or break response in practice, and neither is technical. Roles must be
decided in advance (who declares an incident, who talks to whom, who has the authority to take a
critical system offline), because a crisis is the worst possible time to be working out the org
chart. And communication must be planned: internal coordination, and the harder question
of what you tell customers, regulators, and the public, and when.

## Business continuity and disaster recovery

Two related disciplines sit alongside incident response, and people constantly confuse them.

Business continuity is the big picture: keeping the organisation running during a serious
disruption of any kind, not just a cyberattack. If the office floods, the payroll system dies,
or ransomware locks every file, how does the business keep serving customers and paying staff? It
covers people, processes, premises, and priorities, the whole organisation's ability to function
through a crisis.

Disaster recovery is the technical subset of that: restoring the IT systems and data after
they've been lost or damaged. Business continuity asks "how does the company keep operating?";
disaster recovery asks "how do we get the systems and data back?" Recovery serves continuity (you
restore the systems so the business can continue), but they are not the same plan, and an
organisation needs both.

## The two numbers that drive the plan: RTO and RPO

Disaster recovery turns on two deceptively simple targets, and getting them straight is the most
useful thing in this chapter.

Recovery Time Objective (RTO) is the target elapsed time for restoring
an agreed service level after disruption. Set it within the organisation's
maximum tolerable disruption, allowing for dependencies and validation. A trading platform's RTO might be
minutes; an internal wiki's might be days.

Recovery Point Objective (RPO) is how much data you can afford to lose: the maximum
tolerable gap between your last good backup and the moment of failure. An RPO of one hour means
you can lose at most an hour's work; an RPO of a day means losing a day's is acceptable.

The RPO constrains your recovery design, including backup or replication
frequency and the availability of usable recovery points. This is where plans contradict
themselves. If a company declares an RPO of one hour but backs up only once a night, the plan is
broken on its face: a failure just before the nightly backup could lose nearly twenty-four hours
of data, not one. The stated goal and the actual practice don't match, and no one notices until
the day it matters. Test the gap using a failure time near the end of the backup interval.
A copied but corrupt or compromised backup does not meet the target.
NIST's contingency-planning guidance
helps distinguish recovery objectives from tolerable disruption.

## Backups and alternate sites

Backups support recovery. The 3-2-1 rule of thumb means three copies,
on two types of media, with one off-site. Geographic separation protects
against a local disaster. Offline or appropriately immutable protection
addresses a different problem: an attacker modifying or deleting backups.

An off-site copy reachable with the same compromised administrator account
may still be lost. Separate administration, retention controls and tested
restoration matter. Check that recovery documentation and required keys remain
available during an outage. Neither a backup job's success message nor a
provider's promise substitutes for an observed restore test.

Good backups can reduce pressure to pay for decryption. They do not erase
data already stolen, resolve legal obligations or guarantee a clean recovery.
CISA's ransomware guide
covers prevention, data theft and recovery together.

For systems that can't tolerate much downtime, recovery relies on an alternate site, a second
location ready to take over. These come in flavours by cost and readiness: a hot site is
fully equipped and running, ready in minutes; a cold site is just space and power you'd have
to build out, cheap but slow; a warm site sits in between. Which you choose is a risk-and-cost
decision: the cost-benefit reasoning of the previous chapter, applied to recovery.

## The clock and the crowd

A response plan needs separate owners for technical recovery, communication
and legal assessment. Do not promise that “every breach has a 72-hour clock”.
Under GDPR, the controller's regulator-notification deadline generally runs
from awareness of a personal-data breach, with a risk-based exception;
communication to affected people has a different threshold. Australia's
NDB scheme uses its own coverage, serious-harm and assessment rules.
Web Security and Data Protection
sets out the distinction and primary sources.

Record when relevant facts became known and escalate promptly to the
responsible adviser. Investigation may continue while notification duties
are addressed. Customers need accurate information about what is known,
what is uncertain and what they should do.

A ransom demand adds pressure. Payment does not guarantee restored data or
deletion of stolen copies, and legal restrictions may apply. The authorised
decision-makers need technical and legal advice for the actual circumstances.
This book's exercises do not ask the reader to negotiate or decide a real
ransom payment.

## Testing the plan

One rule outweighs all the rest: an untested plan is only a hope. Recovery plans fail at the
moment they're needed for depressingly ordinary reasons: the backups were running but never
restored-tested and turned out to be corrupt; the recovery documentation was itself on the system
that went down; the one person who knew the process had left. Every one of these is caught by
rehearsing: restoring from backups for real, running tabletop exercises, walking the team
through the crisis while it's still hypothetical. The organisations that recover smoothly are the
ones that practised; the ones that discover their plan's flaws during the real disaster paid the
worst possible price for the lesson.

Incident Zero has two modules that live in this chapter. Incident Response races you to
uncover a hidden attack chain before time and budget run out; Disaster Recovery drops you
into a live breach with a 72-hour notification clock and the ransom decision on the table.
Reading about incident response is one thing; running one against a clock is another. →
incidentzero.retroverse.studio

## Where this connects

This chapter is what you do when a risk from risk management happens anyway, and the hot,
warm, and cold-site choice is that chapter's cost-benefit reasoning applied. Investigation
starts alongside containment; its evidence and attribution methods are taken up in
cybercrime, botnets and forensics. And the breach-notification obligations here are grounded
in the data-protection law of web security and data protection.

## Questions to consider

1. What's the difference between business continuity and disaster recovery? Give an
   example of each.
2. A company backs up nightly. Their RPO is "no more than one hour of data lost." What's wrong,
   and what would you change?
3. Why do so many disaster-recovery plans fail at the moment they're needed? What single
   practice would most reduce that?

preservation alongside containment and separate treatment of exported data.

# Network Security

> What an observer can learn from traffic depends on where they can see it and how it is protected.

Harbour's booking service depends on several network paths: customers reach
the web application, staff use a VPN, and the application queries a database.
Some traffic crosses infrastructure the centre does not control. An observer
on a path may learn from traffic without compromising either endpoint.
This chapter explains those observation limits and how to protect a connection.

## What this chapter covers

By the end you should be able to:

- Explain how scanning and sniffing work and what they reveal.
- Describe what a packet capture shows, and why unencrypted traffic is so revealing.
- Describe common network attacks (spoofing, man-in-the-middle) and their defences.
- Explain why encryption in transit matters, and what segmentation and monitoring add.

## A small network primer

A host is a device or system connected to a network. Its IP address
helps route packets to it; a port helps identify a service on that host.
A service is a listening application, such as a web server. DNS maps
names to addresses. A switch connects devices locally; a router moves traffic
between networks. A subnet groups addresses for routing purposes.

A trust boundary is where rules about access or trust change, such as
between guest Wi-Fi and staff systems. A line on a drawing does not enforce
that boundary: routing, filtering and access controls must implement it.

## The network as an attack surface

Recall the phases of an attack from the first chapter: reconnaissance, then scanning, then
gaining access. Networks are where the first two of those live. Before an attacker can exploit a
system, they have to find it and learn what it's running. A network, by its nature, answers
questions. Ask a machine "are you there?" and, unless told otherwise, it politely replies. Ask
"what services are you running?" and its open ports announce them. Each reachable service adds an exposure to examine. Defaults vary, and a lack
of response does not prove a device or service is absent.

## Scanning: mapping the target

Scanning is how an attacker (or a defender auditing their own network) turns a vague target
into a map. It usually happens in layers. First, find the live hosts: a sweep that asks every
address on a network "are you there?" and notes who answers. Then, for each live host, find the
open ports. A port is a numbered doorway, and each open one usually corresponds to a running
service (a web server, a file share, a remote-login service). Finally, probe those services to
learn which software and version is behind them, because a known version can be matched against
known vulnerabilities from the software-security chapter.

The result is a blueprint: here are your machines, here are the doors that are open, here's the
software behind each one. To an attacker, that's a shopping list. To a defender, the same scan is
an audit. That is why the tools are dual-use, and why the defensive instinct is to *close the
doors you don't need*. Every service you're not using is a door you can shut, shrinking the
blueprint an attacker gets to work from.

## Sniffing: reading the wire

Scanning finds the doors. Sniffing reads what goes through them. A network interface normally
ignores traffic not addressed to it, but put it into a listening ("promiscuous") mode and it will
capture everything it can hear: a packet capture of the raw conversations crossing the
wire. A tool can decode supported protocols from the captured packets.

The phrase it can hear matters. On a switched network, another host's
unicast traffic may never reach your capture interface. Promiscuous mode
does not make the switch send it there. Wi-Fi capture also depends on the
adapter, access-point settings and link protection. Explain the observation
point before claiming what a capture reveals.
Wireshark's Ethernet guidance
sets out these limits.

One thing lands hardest the first time someone sees it: unencrypted application traffic can expose its contents to a listener at
a suitable capture point. Older protocols were designed in a more trusting
era and send everything unencrypted: the web's original HTTP, file transfer with FTP, remote login
with Telnet, some email protocols. Capture that traffic and you don't just see that a
conversation happened. You see its contents: the pages requested, the files moved, and the
usernames and passwords typed in the clear. This is the sniffing that the authentication
chapter warned about, made concrete: the strongest password in the world is worthless if it
crosses the network in plain text for anyone listening to read. It is unsettling to witness, and
it converts "you should use encryption" from advice into an obvious necessity.

## Attacks in the middle

Listening is passive. Network attacks get more dangerous when the attacker stops observing and
starts interfering.

Spoofing is forging an identity on the network: sending traffic that claims to come from an
address that isn't yours. An attacker might spoof a trusted machine's address to slip past a
filter, or forge the source of an attack to hide their tracks. The network's willingness to
believe what packets claim about themselves is the weakness being exploited.

The more powerful move is the man-in-the-middle (or on-path) attack: the attacker positions
themselves between two parties, so that everything each side sends passes through them first.
If the protocol lacks effective authenticated protection, they may read or
alter the conversation: changing a bank account number in
transit, injecting malicious content, or capturing credentials as they flow past. Two common
techniques get an attacker into that position on a local network. ARP spoofing tricks nearby
machines into routing their traffic through the attacker by lying about which hardware address
belongs to which machine. DNS spoofing answers a name lookup with the wrong address, so the
victim connects to the attacker's server while believing it's the real one. The details matter
less than the shape: the attacker exploits the small, trusting mechanisms that make a network
convenient, and turns them into a wiretap.

## Why the local attacker is special

Notice that ARP spoofing and casual sniffing both require the attacker to be *on the same
network*. This is why an attacker who has gained a foothold on your local network, or who is
sharing the coffee-shop Wi-Fi, can do things a purely remote attacker cannot. They can hear the
local chatter, impersonate local machines, and slip into the middle of local conversations in ways
that someone out on the wider internet, several routers away, cannot manage. It's a concrete
reason the inside of a network can never be treated as automatically safe. This is the
assume-breach mindset applied to your own LAN.

## Defences: making the wire private again

The good news is that the central weakness, traffic readable by anyone listening, has a decisive
fix, and you already met it.

Encryption in transit is the answer to sniffing. When traffic is encrypted (the web's HTTPS,
which is HTTP wrapped in the TLS scheme from the cryptography chapter), an eavesdropper still
captures the packets but sees only unreadable ciphertext. The credentials, the page contents, the
files: all gibberish to anyone without the key. It protects the encrypted contents, but packet timing, sizes and some addressing
information remain observable. Protocol and configuration choices determine
what else is visible. TLS must also authenticate the intended endpoint to
resist an active impostor; ignoring certificate errors undermines that check.
That is why the migration of the web from HTTP to HTTPS was one of the most important security
improvements of the last decade, and why sending anything sensitive over an unencrypted protocol
is now indefensible.

Two more layers back it up. Segmentation divides a network into zones with controlled routes
between them, so that a foothold in one part doesn't grant free movement across the whole. It's
the same idea that starves a worm (from the malware chapter) and contains an incident (from the
planning chapter). Monitoring watches network traffic for the tell-tale signs of trouble (a
host scanning its neighbours, traffic to a known-bad address, a volume spike), so that the
intrusion you couldn't prevent is at least detected, feeding straight into the incident response
of the previous chapter. Encryption hides the contents, segmentation limits the movement, and
monitoring catches what gets through. No single one is enough.

## Play it

Incident Zero's Network Building module makes you buy an architecture you can't fully
afford, and the gaps you leave come back to bite in later rounds. It's a quick, concrete
lesson in why security is a budget problem as much as a technical one. →
incidentzero.retroverse.studio

## Where this connects

The credentials a sniffer harvests are the ones authentication taught you to protect, and
encryption in transit is what stops them leaking. That encryption is the hybrid TLS scheme from
cryptography, in action. And the next chapter, VPN and firewalls, covers the two
defences that build on this one: a firewall to control what traffic is allowed, and a VPN to
protect traffic within its configured tunnel.

# VPN & Firewalls

> A firewall decides what traffic to let through. Writing a good rule is harder than it sounds, and that difficulty is the lesson.

The last chapter left us with two problems: networks let attackers reach systems they
shouldn't, and they let attackers read traffic they shouldn't. This chapter is about the two
classic defences that answer those problems. A firewall controls what traffic is allowed to
pass. It decides who gets to reach what. A VPN protects traffic in transit. It makes the
readable wire private again. Both are everyday tools, and both are widely misunderstood.
Understanding what each does and does not do is worth more than knowing how to configure either.

## What this chapter covers

By the end you should be able to:

- Explain what a firewall does and the difference between packet-filtering, stateful, and
  application-layer firewalls.
- Reason about firewall rules: default-deny vs default-allow, and why order matters.
- Explain what a VPN provides (confidentiality and integrity over an untrusted network) and
  what it does not.
- Describe defence in depth in a network context: no single control trusted alone.

## What a firewall does

A firewall is a checkpoint. It sits at a boundary between networks (classically between a
trusted internal network and the untrusted internet) and inspects the traffic trying to cross,
allowing what's permitted and blocking the rest. That's the whole idea: a gate with a guard who
checks each arrival against a set of rules. A firewall does not try to block everything. Firewalls
run at network boundaries, on individual hosts, and increasingly in cloud environments, but the
job is always the same: decide, packet by packet or connection by connection, what's allowed
through.

The difficulty, and the reason this chapter exists, is that the guard is only as good as the
rules they're given. A firewall doesn't understand intent; it applies your policy literally. So
the security a firewall provides is the quality of the thinking that went into its rules. It's a
recurring truth in this book: the tool is easy, the judgement is hard.

## The three kinds of firewall

Firewalls differ in how deeply they look at traffic, and the progression is worth knowing because
each level trades speed for insight.

A packet-filtering firewall is the simplest: it examines each packet's headers in isolation
(source and destination address, port, protocol) and permits or blocks based on those alone. It's
fast, but it has no memory. Because it looks at each packet on its own, it can't tell whether a
packet is a legitimate reply to a request you made or an unsolicited probe from an attacker; both
look the same in isolation.

A stateful firewall fixes that by tracking the state of connections. It remembers that you
opened a connection to a web server, so it knows to allow that server's replies back in, while
still blocking unsolicited inbound traffic you never asked for. This connection awareness makes it
more useful, and it's the modern default for most purposes.

An application-layer firewall (or proxy) goes deeper still: it understands the actual
protocol (the structure of an HTTP request, for instance) and can make decisions based on the
content, not just the addresses. A web application firewall that blocks a malicious-looking
request is working at this level. Deeper inspection has processing and configuration costs. Capabilities and
performance depend on implementation, and encrypted content may be unavailable
unless the design explicitly provides authorised inspection.

## Writing the rules

The single most important decision in a firewall policy is its default stance, and it comes
in two flavours. Default-deny blocks everything and permits only what you explicitly allow: a
guest list. Default-allow permits everything and blocks only what you explicitly forbid: a
banned list. Default-deny limits unexpected access at a defined boundary: with a banned list you can only block the threats you thought of, and
attackers make their living on the ones you didn't. A guest list fails safe: anything unforeseen
is denied by default. A banned list fails open, letting the unforeseen straight through. The
trade-off is friction: default-deny means every legitimate need must be anticipated and allowed,
which is more work and more support tickets. Test necessary service use as well as prohibited access.

Two more subtleties bite people constantly. Order matters: in the first-match model used for our paper exercise, rules are evaluated top
to bottom, so a broad "allow" placed above a specific "deny"
silently defeats the deny. The packet matches the allow first and is waved through before the
deny is ever considered. And specificity matters: one rule written too broadly can open a
hole nobody notices, and a large rule set becomes so complex that mistakes hide in it. The cost
of a bad rule is not abstract; a single misordered or over-broad line is how real networks end up
exposed while everyone believes the firewall has them covered.

## VPNs: a private tunnel over a public network

A firewall controls what can pass. A VPN (virtual private network) protects *what's already
passing*. It solves the plaintext problem from the last chapter by building an encrypted
tunnel between configured endpoints. Traffic selected for that tunnel is
protected until it reaches the other endpoint. A full-tunnel configuration and
split tunnelling cover different traffic; do not assume every application,
DNS request or fallback path is included. To anyone listening in between (the
attacker on the coffee-shop Wi-Fi, the operator of an untrusted network), your traffic is just
ciphertext. The VPN gives you confidentiality and integrity across a network you don't trust,
which is what an untrusted wire otherwise denies you.

A secure VPN protocol authenticates peers, establishes keys and protects
selected traffic against disclosure and undetected alteration. The mechanisms
vary by protocol and configuration; some use pre-shared credentials. The cryptography chapter explains why authentication and integrity matter
alongside confidentiality.

## What a VPN does not do

VPNs are also the most over-sold tool in consumer security, so it's worth being precise about
their limits.

A VPN protects the link between you and the VPN server. It does not make you anonymous: your
traffic still emerges from the VPN server toward its real destination, and that destination still
knows what you did there. It does not secure your device: malware already on your machine is
unaffected by an encrypted tunnel. And it does not protect the leg beyond the VPN server. If
that onward traffic is unencrypted, it's exposed again once it leaves the tunnel. A VPN is not a substitute for HTTPS. Whether a separate VPN is useful depends
on the access requirement and threat model. A VPN also doesn't remove trust; it relocates
it. You've stopped trusting the local network and started trusting the VPN provider, which sees routing information and any unencrypted onward
traffic. Properly protected HTTPS content remains encrypted to the website;
leaving the VPN tunnel does not itself reveal that content. That can be a good trade on hostile Wi-Fi and a bad one with a shady provider,
but "a VPN makes you safe" is a marketing line rather than a security fact.

## Defence in depth, again

Consider what each tool leaves outside its scope. A firewall controls
what traffic is allowed but can't judge the intent behind traffic it permits. Malware happily
tunnels out over the same port 443 your firewall allows for ordinary web browsing. A VPN protects
traffic in transit but does nothing about what happens at either end. So they join the layers from
the last chapter: the firewall decides what's allowed, the VPN protects what's in motion,
segmentation limits how far a foothold spreads, and monitoring catches the malicious traffic that
slips through a permitted door. Check that these controls fail independently where possible, and test the
paths between them. More layers do not help if they share the same unchecked
administrator access.

## Where this connects

A firewall and a VPN are the two defences against the scanning, sniffing, and man-in-the-middle
attacks of the previous chapter, network security. A secure tunnel applies the key and authentication ideas from cryptography. And the monitoring that backs up a firewall feeds the
detection stage of incident response in incident and disaster planning.

## Questions to consider

1. Should a firewall default to deny or default to allow? Argue the trade-off, and say
   which you'd choose for a company network and why.
2. A VPN "makes you anonymous / makes you safe." What does it actually protect, and what does it
   not?
3. Give one example of a second layer catching something a firewall alone would miss.

In 15 minutes, trace all seven flows in Harbour's paper firewall exercise.
Explain the rule that decides each one. Repair the policy and repeat the
tests, including legitimate service use. Success means four expected allows
and three expected denies, with no broad rule defeating a later restriction.
This notation is for reasoning only, not deployment.

# Web Security & Data Protection

> A web request should expose only the data and actions its user is authorised to access.

Harbour lets customers view bookings through a browser. A mistake in how
the application handles a request could expose someone else's information.
This chapter connects web weaknesses to access controls and personal-data
responsibilities. A vulnerability can lead to a privacy incident; discovering a flaw alone
does not establish that personal data was compromised or notification is required.

## What this chapter covers

By the end you should be able to:

- Explain the most common web vulnerabilities (injection, XSS, broken authentication,
  session issues) at a working level.
- Trace how a web attack unfolds, step by step.
- Explain core data-protection obligations (privacy principles and mandatory breach
  notification) with the major regimes as examples.
- Connect web weaknesses to the data-protection consequences when they're exploited.

## Why the web is the front line

A web application is, almost by definition, exposed: it exists to accept requests from strangers
over the internet. Every input field, URL, and uploaded file is a place where untrusted data
enters your system, and the browser on the other end is a universal client you don't control. One recurring weakness is trusting input that should never be trusted.
Another is failing to check what an authenticated user is allowed to do. Data arriving from a user is treated as if it were safe, and the
attacker's craft is to make that data do something it was never meant to.

## Injection: when data becomes commands

The archetype is injection, and its most famous form is SQL injection. A web application
builds a database query using something the user typed, a search term or a login name. If the
application glues the user's input into the query text, an attacker can type input that becomes
part of the command itself. Instead of a username they submit a fragment that
changes what the query does: turning "find the user named X" into "find every user," or worse,
"delete the table." The database, unable to tell the developer's intent from the attacker's
addition, obeys.

Injection generalises beyond databases. Any time untrusted input is fed into something that
interprets commands (a database, the operating system, a template engine), the same flaw can
appear. And the defence generalises too: keep data and code separate. Rather than build a
command by pasting text together, use mechanisms (parameterised queries, prepared statements)
that hand the input to the system explicitly as data, so it can never be mistaken for
instructions. Validate what comes in, and give the application only the database privileges it
needs, so a successful injection does less damage.

## Cross-site scripting: running attacker code in your browser

Cross-site scripting (XSS) is injection aimed at the other users of a site rather than its
database. It is worth understanding because it explains how an attacker ends up running code in
your browser without ever touching your machine.

A site lets users submit content (a comment, a profile, a message) and
displays it back to other users. If the site fails to treat that content as untrusted, an
attacker can submit a small script instead of ordinary text. When another user views the page,
their browser receives that script as part of the trusted site's page and runs it in the
site's context, with the victim's session. Now the attacker's code, executing as if it were the
site itself, may read an accessible session token, act as them, redirect them, or deface
what they see. The victim did nothing wrong except visit a page they had every reason to trust.
The defence mirrors injection: treat all user content as untrusted, and
encode it for the output context so the browser treats it as data.
HTML text, attributes, URLs and scripts have different rules; safe framework
facilities and sanitisation are needed where rich content is deliberately allowed.
OWASP's XSS guidance
explains why one universal escaping function is insufficient.

## Authentication and session weaknesses

The web has a structural quirk that creates its own class of attacks. The protocol underneath is
stateless: each request stands alone, with no memory of the last. So after you log in, the
server issues a session token (usually stored in a cookie) that your browser sends with every
subsequent request to say "it's still me." That token is your authenticated session. Anyone who
steals it can impersonate you without ever knowing your password. This
is session hijacking, and it's why the sniffing and XSS attacks in this book are so dangerous:
they're often after the token rather than the password. A related trick, session fixation, has the
attacker plant a session identifier they already know, then wait for the victim to log in under
it. The defences are concrete: send tokens only over HTTPS so they can't be sniffed, flag cookies
so scripts can't read them and they don't leak, issue a fresh token at login, and expire and revoke sessions so a stolen one does not remain useful indefinitely.
An HttpOnly cookie prevents JavaScript reading that cookie; injected code may
still cause authenticated actions. Cookie flags do not make an XSS flaw harmless.

## A valid login is not permission

Suppose Harbour's customer page accepts a booking identifier. A customer
changes that identifier and receives another person's booking. The login may
be genuine and the query parameter safely handled; the missing check is
whether this customer is allowed to access that record.

Enforce ownership and role checks server-side on every relevant request.
Hiding a button is not access control. Test both a permitted action and an
attempt by a different account that should be denied.
OWASP's access-control guidance
describes this distinction.

## The other side: protecting the data

Behind every one of these attacks is data. Increasingly it is personal data, and the law now
requires its protection. Modern data-protection law sets out principles for
handling personal information that recur across jurisdictions: collect only what you need and only
lawfully; use it only for the purpose you stated; keep it accurate; secure it; don't retain
it longer than necessary; and let people see and correct what you hold about them. Australia
codifies these as the Australian Privacy Principles (APPs) under its Privacy Act; the EU's
GDPR is another version of the same pattern; other countries have their own. The names differ;
the shape is consistent. The point for a defender is that *security is
written into the law*. The applicable duties depend on the organisation, data and jurisdiction.
A breach warrants investigation; its occurrence alone does not establish
that every relevant legal duty was violated.

### When it breaks: breach notification

A technical finding starts an assessment, not an automatic notification
verdict. Identify the information, the affected people, what occurred, and
which laws cover the organisation. Record uncertainty and involve the
responsible privacy adviser promptly.

Australia: NDB scheme. For covered entities, an eligible breach generally
involves unauthorised access or disclosure, or loss likely to lead to it,
where serious harm to an individual is likely. Successful remedial action
and exceptions can affect notification. Suspected eligible breaches need
assessment: take all reasonable steps to complete it within 30 calendar
days. Once there are reasonable grounds to believe an eligible breach
occurred, notify the OAIC and relevant individuals as soon as practicable.
Thirty days is not a general waiting period.
OAIC's guidance

EU: GDPR. A controller notifies the supervisory authority without undue
delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours of becoming aware of a
personal-data breach, unless it is unlikely to pose a risk to people's
rights and freedoms. A likely high risk generally requires communication
to affected individuals without undue delay, subject to exceptions.
Processors notify the controller without undue delay. Document breaches
and decisions even where regulator notification is not required.
EDPB's notification guidance

These educational summaries were checked in September 2026. They are not a
complete statement of the law; other sectoral or contractual obligations
may also apply. For a real incident, use the current rules and authorised
legal advice.

## From a web flaw to a decision

A report that one customer can read another's booking establishes an access
control problem. Logs showing an unauthorised bulk export add evidence of
exposure. Whether notification is required still depends on coverage,
information involved, risk or harm thresholds and remedial action.

At Harbour, preserve the export evidence and escalate to the privacy adviser.
Do not equate a successful restore with resolution of disclosure, or a lack
of actor attribution with a lack of harm. The task is to make a supported,
timely decision while technical investigation continues.

## Where this connects

This chapter connects to several others. Authentication is central because the session tokens
attacked here are how the web remembers you are logged in, and stealing one sidesteps the password.
Network security matters because sniffing and man-in-the-middle attacks are common ways session
tokens get stolen, which is why HTTPS matters. Incident & disaster planning shares the
breach-notification clock here, the same one that drives a real breach response.

1. In plain words, how does a cross-site scripting attack end up running an attacker's code
   in your browser, on a site you trust?
2. A company is breached and customer data leaks. Under a mandatory breach-notification scheme,
   what are they obliged to do, and why does that obligation exist at all?

   just your username, and what, if anything, can they still not do?

# Cybercrime, Botnets & Forensics

> Many attacks are a business. Understanding who profits, and how, helps explain the choices defenders face.

Many financially motivated attacks involve a market of specialists selling
access, malware and services. Understanding that market helps explain why
a compromised ordinary device can matter. Other adversaries may seek
espionage, disruption or personal harm, so financial incentives are one
lens, not a complete threat model.

This chapter uses the malware vocabulary from chapter 2, then concentrates
on evidence: what an observation supports, what it merely suggests, and
what additional information an investigator would need.

## What this chapter covers

By the end you should be able to:

- Explain what a botnet is, and how one is built and monetised.
- Describe the cybercrime economy: the division of labour behind modern attacks.
- Explain, at a high level, how a real botnet works (credential-stealing and IoT examples).
- Connect forensic traces to the attribution of an attack, and explain chain of custody.

## Cybercrime is a business

The single most useful shift in thinking is to treat serious cybercrime as commerce. Attackers
have costs, revenues, competitors, and customers. They reinvest profits, offer support, build
reputations, and specialise. Specialisation is more efficient, just as in any
legitimate economy. Cryptocurrency can help move value across borders, but public-ledger
transactions may also provide an investigative trail. Traceability depends
on the system and available evidence. The result is a mature underground market where almost any capability can be bought.

Seeing crime as business reframes defence. You don't have to make an attack impossible. For opportunistic profit-driven attacks, raising costs and reducing returns
can discourage targeting. That does not guarantee deterrence. Raise the attacker's cost, lower their expected return, and
much of the volume moves to an easier target. A great deal of security is about not being the
cheapest victim on the market.

## The division of labour

The defining feature of the modern cybercrime economy is that participants can specialise rather than doing the whole job. The work is split among specialists, each selling to the next. Malware developers write
and sell the tools, often as polished kits with updates and customer support. Initial access
brokers specialise in breaking in, then sell the foothold*, access to an already-compromised
company, to whoever wants to use it. Infrastructure providers rent out "bulletproof" hosting
and the command servers that run attacks. Ransomware-as-a-service operators lease their
ransomware to affiliates, who run the attacks and hand back a cut. Money mules and launderers
turn stolen funds into clean, spendable cash.

This specialisation has three consequences, and they explain why cybercrime is so stubborn. It
lowers the barrier to entry: you no longer need to be a skilled hacker to launch an attack,
you can rent the capability. It makes the ecosystem resilient: arrest one malware author and
the access brokers, hosts, and launderers all carry on, and a competitor fills the gap. And it
muddies attribution: with so many hands touching an attack, working out who is
responsible is hard. A disruption may leave other participants able to rebuild or reuse parts
of the operation.

## Botnets: crime at scale

Chapter 2 introduced a botnet: compromised devices under remote control
through a command-and-control channel. Here the important distinction is
between a device's own data and its value as infrastructure. A device can
supply traffic, computing capacity or a relay even when its owner believes
it has nothing worth stealing.

A single device can still be valuable, for example because of its accounts
or network position. Scale adds other uses such as distributed denial of
service. A defender should consider both individual compromise and the
harm a fleet can cause to others.

## How they work: two patterns

Two well-known cases show the pattern without needing any perishable statistics.

Gameover Zeus, a member of the Zeus family, was used for credential theft
and fraud. The FBI's 2014 account of its disruption
shows why a response can involve investigators, service providers and affected
device owners. Disrupting infrastructure and cleaning infected devices are
different parts of the work.

Mirai recruited internet-connected devices, including cameras and routers,
using weak or default credentials. The
US Department of Justice's account of the 2017 guilty pleas
provides a historical source. For a defender, unmanaged devices and default
credentials belong in the asset inventory even when their owners do not
think of them as computers.

## Following the trail: forensics and attribution

When an attack succeeds, the work shifts to investigation: digital forensics, the discipline
of reconstructing what happened from the traces left behind. And attackers leave more traces than
they'd like. System and network logs may record activity. File timestamps and
document metadata can suggest a sequence, but can be modified. Some
email headers are trustworthy only as far as the systems that added
them; sender-supplied fields can be forged. Check clock offsets, collection
context and corroborating observations. From fragments like these an investigator builds a timeline: a
defensible account of who did what, and when.

Two ideas make forensics a discipline rather than a rummage. The first is chain of custody:
evidence handling should make integrity and provenance open to examination: who touched it,
when, and how, in an unbroken record. Poor handling can undermine confidence and admissibility. A digest shows
whether a captured item changed relative to a recorded value; it does not
prove the original item was truthful or identify its author. (This is the hard lesson the
game module makes you feel.) The second is that attribution is difficult. The same
division of labour and the same botnets that power attacks also hide the people behind them:
traffic routed through a botnet appears to come from innocent victims, infrastructure is rented
and disposable, and false clues are sometimes planted deliberately. Careful defenders talk about
attribution in terms of confidence, not certainty.

Choose the next investigative step for its relevance and reliability, not
because one kind of trace is always the clue attackers forget. Preserve
the supplied material and document collection, access and interpretation.
Investigation supports containment and recovery as well as later attribution;
it need not wait until systems are restored.
NIST's incident-response guidance
provides the wider operational context.

# Human Factors & Emerging Trends

> A useful defence makes mistakes easier to report and less costly to survive.

Security depends on people using and maintaining systems under ordinary
working pressures. A login prompt, an urgent request or an unclear warning
can put the wrong decision within easy reach. The design question is what
happens next: can the person get help, and do other controls limit the harm?

This chapter brings together usable procedures, independent verification and
a cautious assessment of AI's effects. The final chapter applies those ideas
to the complete defender's dossier.

## What this chapter covers

By the end you should be able to:

- Explain how human decisions interact with system design, and why blame
  alone does not repair a weakness.
- Describe social engineering and phishing, and the defences that actually work.
- Reason about how non-technical people make security decisions with the information they're
  given.
- Discuss emerging trends, including AI's double role as both a tool for attackers and a
  tool for defenders.

## The weakest link, and why blaming the user fails

Calling people the weakest link treats a complex system as a single chain.
More usefully, ask what the interface, permissions and work process made likely.
The claim that "the users are the weakest link" is a trap: a framing that excuses bad
security. When a well-crafted phishing email fools an employee, the instinct is to blame the
employee: they should have known better. But look closer. They were doing their job, quickly,
inside a system that made the wrong action easy, the right action unclear, and the difference
between the two nearly invisible. Blaming the person lets the system off the hook. A team that
blames users stops fixing the thing they could fix.

A more useful stance examines the system. People will make mistakes. That is a constant, and good
security is designed around it rather than trying to train it away. This is assume breach
applied to human beings: assume that, sooner or later, someone will be fooled, and build so the
organisation survives it. That single shift, from "make users perfect" to "make mistakes
survivable", is the difference between security that works and security that just apportions
blame after the fact.

## Social engineering and phishing

Social engineering is hacking the human instead of the machine: manipulating a person into
doing what the attacker wants. It works because it targets the mental shortcuts we all rely on to
get through the day. An attacker invokes authority ("this is IT, we need your password"),
urgency ("the account will be closed in an hour"), fear, trust, or plain
helpfulness. The victim, trying to be efficient and cooperative, complies. They are usually
behaving well by the ordinary rules of work; the attacker has pointed those good instincts in
the wrong direction.

Phishing is social engineering at scale: the deceptive email or message, cast wide, that
lures people into revealing credentials or running malware. Spear-phishing is the targeted,
personalised version aimed at a specific person, and it is more convincing. These attacks succeed
because they exploit how humans are built to work. Their victims are not foolish.

The defences that help are instructive, because the obvious one is the weakest. Awareness
training matters, but you cannot train people to be perfect forever, and a defence that
requires every human to be vigilant every time will eventually fail. The stronger approach is to
reduce the reliance on human vigilance: use technical controls that make a mistake less
catastrophic. Multi-factor authentication means a phished password alone isn't enough, which is
the point of the authentication chapter. Email filtering can remove some lures before they reach a person.
Firm verification procedures for high-risk actions mean no single email can move money or hand
over data without an independent check. Best of all is to make the safe path the easy path,
so that doing the secure thing is also the convenient thing. The goal is a system in which an
ordinary human error does not become a disaster, rather than a workforce that never errs.

## Communicating risk to people who aren't technical

A quieter human-factors failure is that we push security decisions onto people who have no way
to make them well. The classic example is the browser certificate warning. When your browser
says a site's certificate can't be trusted, it is telling you something important. It could not
verify you're talking to who you think you are, using the identity-proving cryptography from the
cryptography chapter. That warning might be the only thing standing between you and a
man-in-the-middle attacker.

Some users bypass warnings when the language is unclear, the warning interrupts
urgent work, or past experience has taught them to discount it. Behaviour
varies with the warning and context; do not assume most users always ignore it. This
is a design failure, not the user's. A consequential security decision has been handed to
someone without the information or context to make it, in words that mean nothing to them. Good
security gives people clear information and, wherever possible, doesn't force impossible
judgements on them.

## Emerging trends: AI on both sides

AI can assist both attackers and defenders, but capability claims need a
date, a task and evidence. It can lower the effort of drafting messages,
summarising data or producing code. That does not make every message flawless,
every exploit effective or an operation limitless.

For attackers, plausible personalised language and synthetic audio can make
familiar verification shortcuts less reliable. For defenders, models may help
triage reports and interpret logs, but false alarms, missed attacks and
untrusted inputs still need evaluation. Logs and messages can contain hostile
instructions as well as sensitive information, so an assistant must not be
given authority merely because it produced a confident answer.

The NCSC assessment of AI and cyber threats to 2027
describes expected changes in attacker capability. Treat it as a dated
assessment with uncertainty, not proof that every forecast has occurred.

At Harbour, an apparent manager's voice is not enough to approve an account
reset or bulk export. Use a known contact route and the agreed approval
process. This remains useful whether the message was written by a person
or generated by a model. Give staff a reporting path that does not punish
them for raising a concern that turns out to be harmless.

## Where this connects

This chapter connects back across everything before it. Human decisions influence the design, operation and recovery of technical
controls, so human factors belongs throughout the book.
Authentication is the clearest case: MFA is a technical control that makes a human mistake
survivable. And that same thread, the human running through every stage, is part of how the next
chapter ties the whole book into a single lifecycle.

## Questions to consider

1. "Users are the weakest link." Argue against this framing: whose fault is a successful
   phish, really?
2. What does a browser certificate warning tell you? Explain why someone might
   bypass it and propose a safer route the organisation could offer.
3. Give one way AI helps attackers and one way it helps defenders. Which side do you
   think it favours, and why?

## Make reporting usable

In 10 minutes, write a response to this fictional message: “Mara here.
Approve the login prompt now so I can finish the bookings.” Use a known
contact route and the agreed approval process. Add a non-blaming report
for someone who already approved it. Success means prompt escalation and
independent verification, without using contact details supplied by the message.

# The Long Game

> A defence plan should make its limits as visible as its controls.

You now have the pieces of a defender's dossier. The final test changes the
budget and recovery target. It asks whether you can recognise an unresolved
risk and explain it to the person authorised to decide. A polished plan
that hides a failed test is less useful than a short one that exposes it.

## What this chapter covers

By the end you should be able to:

- Connect assets, controls, evidence and recovery decisions in one plan.
- Check whether a changed requirement invalidates an earlier answer.
- Distinguish a justified decision from a claim that everything is secure.
- Assign responsibility for unresolved risk and future review.

## Joining the dots

Return to the seven activities introduced in chapter 1: design, harden,
detect, respond, recover, investigate, and audit/learn. Human decisions
run through every activity. These are overlapping tasks, not seven
departments that hand an incident along in a fixed sequence.

At Harbour, permissions influence whether a login can become a bulk export.
Logging affects what the response lead can establish. Recovery credentials
affect whether backups survive. A notification assessment may still be open
after the service is restored. Those connections are what the dossier should
make visible.

A checklist can help an authorised responder avoid missing a necessary step.
It needs an owner, a context and testing. The failure is treating checked
boxes as proof that the service can meet its obligations under changed
conditions.

## Complete the independent case

Use Harbour's weekend programme.
Allow 30–45 minutes, working alone without AI. The appendix supplies every
changed fact and an eight-point check. Write your answer before reading
the comparison.

Your one-page decision needs the assets and permissions, an affordable
control choice, risk arithmetic, allowed and denied tests, both recovery
checks, an evidence-preserving response and a privacy escalation. End with
what is ready, what is not, who can decide, and what evidence is needed next.

You are not required to make the constraints fit. If the stated recovery
time misses the target or the budget leaves a known boundary weakness,
say so. Do not create an imaginary free control to make the answer look
complete. An explicit request for a changed budget, a tested improvement
or a deferred launch is a legitimate result.

## Check and transfer

Compare your answer with the
reasoning check.
Aim for seven of eight criteria, with no unsafe testing, unsupported readiness
claim or missed recovery-target failure. If you disagree with the worked
choice, identify the constraint and evidence that make your alternative
defensible.

For a second attempt, ask another reader to change one supplied constraint.
Predict which section of your dossier must change before recalculating it.
That is a stronger test of understanding than repeating the original answer.

## Play the whole thing

Every stage in that loop is easier to understand than to do. Doing it, under time and
budget pressure, is where the judgement lives. Incident Zero, the free print-and-play game
referenced throughout these chapters, has a full campaign that runs this entire lifecycle end
to end: design a network, harden it, detect and respond to a breach, recover from the disaster,
and investigate what happened. Playing it is the closest thing to living a security cycle in
an afternoon. If this book gave you the map, the campaign lets you walk the ground. →
incidentzero.retroverse.studio

## Questions to consider

1. Which supplied fact most changed your control choice, and why?
2. Which conclusion depends on evidence you do not yet have?
3. What should trigger a new review after an initially acceptable plan?

## Carry the decision forward

Keep the dossier as a record of a decision under stated conditions. When
the system, staff, evidence or obligations change, revisit it. The useful
habit is to explain what a control does, demonstrate it where possible,
and make the remaining uncertainty visible to the right decision-maker.

# Appendix: Setting Up a Practice Environment 

The complete core route is the paper-based
Defender's Dossier. Its observations and rules are
fictional. You can learn the reasoning without scanning a device, cracking
a password or capturing anyone's traffic.

## Permission before practice

For optional hands-on work, obtain explicit written permission for the
systems, accounts, techniques and time window involved. Owning a laptop does
not authorise testing the shared network, a cloud provider or other users.
If scope is unclear, stop and ask the responsible owner.

Use synthetic data and test accounts. This book does not ask you to handle
live malware, attack a public website or upload real logs to an assistant.

## Isolation is a property to check

A VM is a separate guest operating system. A container shares more of its
host's underlying environment. Either can be useful; neither name proves
that a mistake cannot reach real systems.

With VirtualBox, NAT allows outbound access to the host/LAN and internet.
Host-only connects guests to the host. An internal network can connect
selected guests without attaching that network to the host or outside world.
Additional adapters, routing, forwarding and shared resources can change
the boundary. These distinctions are explicit in
Oracle's networking table.

Before using a deliberately vulnerable environment, check every adapter,
published port, shared folder and credential. Keep personal files and
production credentials outside it. Use only an environment whose maintainer
documents the isolation and reset procedure for your platform. A container
with a published port or mounted host directory is not an isolated playground
merely because it starts in a container.

## A safe optional route

Choose a maintained teaching environment with explicit authorisation and
setup instructions. OWASP Juice Shop
is one example of an intentionally insecure training application; read its
own setup and safety documentation before installing anything. Do not treat
its public demonstration or someone else's installation as an authorised target.

Write down the intended boundary, confirm what can reach what using the
environment's documented checks, and verify its reset procedure before an
exercise. If a service is exposed beyond that boundary, a test reveals real
data, or you cannot explain a connection, stop. Disconnect the exercise
environment and ask its maintainer or your instructor for help.

A snapshot helps reset state; it does not remove data that has already escaped.
Record the environment version and your observations so another authorised
learner can reproduce the exercise. Tool-specific installation is deliberately
outside this book; if you cannot establish the boundary, use the paper route.

## Tools by capability

Names provide anchors, not required installations.

| Capability | Examples |
|---|---|
| Inspect authorised host/service exposure | Nmap |
| Study synthetic password hashes | John the Ripper, Hashcat |
| Inspect supplied or authorised packet captures | Wireshark |
| Work with keys and signatures | OpenSSL, GnuPG |
| Test a permitted firewall policy | nftables |
| Examine supplied forensic evidence | Autopsy, Volatility |

Follow the official documentation and scope for the chosen environment.
Successful setup is not the learning outcome: explaining an observation,
its limitations and the defensive decision it supports is.

# The Defender's Dossier 

This is an original, fictional case. All people, records, prices, observations
and risk estimates below are invented for teaching. No real system needs to
be contacted. A notebook is enough; the tables are the complete exercise
inputs, not extracts from a missing lab.

## Harbour Community Centre 

Harbour runs public workshops. Eight staff use a hosted booking application.
It holds 600 participant records: names, contact details and workshop choices.
It holds no payment-card data. A public web service talks to a private booking
database. Staff administer the application through a VPN gateway. Guest Wi-Fi
should provide internet access without access to the database or staff devices.

Mara, the manager, approves bulk exports and account changes. Rae, the
receptionist, needs to view and amend individual bookings, but does not need
bulk export or account-administration rights. Volunteers need only the
attendance list for their assigned workshop. The current system mistakenly
gives every staff account bulk-export permission. Customers should see only
their own booking. A shared administrator password is another current weakness.

The centre director owns the risk decision. Mara coordinates an incident;
the service provider handles technical containment and recovery under an
agreed scope. A designated privacy adviser determines applicable legal duties.
For this exercise assume the centre is covered by Australia's NDB scheme.
There is no assumption that every real small community organisation is covered.

The booking service has a recovery time objective of four hours and a recovery
point objective of one hour. Disruption starts at 09:20 in the exercise.
Backups currently run at 23:00 each night using the application's administrator
credentials. A previous restore test took two hours, excluding containment and
validation. A successful backup job is not evidence of a successful restore.

### A bounded budget

The director can spend \$3,000 per year on additional controls. These are
fictional annual costs including setup amortisation and staff time. Existing
basic application logs and staff reporting remain available with any choice.

| Option | Annual cost | What it changes |
|---|---:|---|
| A: account and access repair | \$1,000 | Individual accounts, administrative phishing-resistant MFA, reviewed permissions, recovery checks and session revocation procedure |
| B: network boundary repair | \$800 | Guest isolation, restricted database paths, reviewed firewall policy and allowed/denied connection tests |
| C: recovery repair | \$1,200 | Hourly recovery points, separate backup administration, off-site immutable copies and scheduled restore tests |
| D: managed alert review | \$1,000 | A staffed alert-review service with an agreed escalation time; does not change access rights or backup protection |

: {tbl-colwidths="[25,15,60]"}

A, B and C cost exactly \$3,000. D cannot be added to that combination within
the budget. The centre must still assign someone to review its existing logs
and reports if it does not buy D. None of the options guarantees prevention.

For a separate expected-loss calculation, use an incident loss of
\$50,000 and an estimated annual occurrence rate of 0.10. Suppose option A
reduces that rate to 0.04, without changing loss per incident. These are
scenario assumptions, not measured product effectiveness.

### A paper firewall model 

Use zone names rather than real addresses. All rows below describe new
connections. For this exercise only, the first matching rule decides; an
unmatched connection is denied. Return traffic for an allowed established
connection is allowed. Do not copy this teaching notation into a firewall.

| Rule | Source | Destination | Service | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Any | Booking database | Any | Allow |
| 2 | Guest | Booking database | Any | Deny |
| 3 | Public | Web | HTTPS | Allow |
| 4 | Web | Booking database | Database | Allow |
| 5 | Staff | VPN gateway | VPN | Allow |

“Any” includes every named zone and service. The four legitimate new flows
are Public to Web using HTTPS, Web to Booking database using Database,
Staff to VPN gateway using VPN, and Guest to Internet using HTTPS.
All other new flows should be denied in this simplified model. DNS and
other supporting flows are deliberately outside the model; a production
policy would need them specified and tested.

### Evidence cards 

These are supplied observations, not a capture or an invitation to investigate
a live service. Application events and the manager's notes share the same
clock in this exercise. Real investigations must check clock offsets.

| Time | Supplied observation |
|---|---|
| 09:10 | Application log records a successful login to Rae's account from a previously unseen network address |
| 09:12 | Application log records a completed export of 600 participant records under Rae's session |
| 09:15 | Rae reports approving an unexpected login prompt after receiving an urgent message |
| 09:20 | Mara declares a suspected incident and suspends booking access; service disruption begins |
| 09:25 | Provider preserves an access-controlled copy of relevant logs, records who collected it and its digest |
| 09:30 | Provider revokes sessions and begins a two-hour restoration rehearsal in an isolated recovery environment |
| 11:30 | Rehearsal restore finishes; technical checks continue |
| 11:50 | Integrity and access checks finish; the rehearsal is accepted |

: {tbl-colwidths="[15,85]"}

The export event supports investigation of personal-data exposure. It does
not identify the person operating the session or establish the destination
or subsequent use of the exported records. A new address alone is not proof
of compromise. The reported prompt approval is relevant evidence, not a
complete explanation. No evidence card proves the source system is clean
or authorises reconnecting it.

## A worked dossier 

Read this after attempting the chapter tasks. It is one defensible answer,
not the only possible policy.

### Protection and access

My first asset is participant information. An account with excess export
rights could expose many records in one action. I choose individual accounts,
restricted exports and an alert on bulk export. The residual risk includes
misuse of a legitimately authorised manager account. A permission test should
show Rae can update a booking but cannot export all records, and that a
volunteer cannot view another workshop's attendance list.

Availability matters when people arrive for a class. Integrity matters when
a booking or contact detail changes. Encryption in transit and at rest helps
protect disclosure through particular paths; it does not prevent an authorised
application session from exporting readable data. A hash stored next to an
editable export is not independent proof that the export is genuine. A trusted
digest or signature must be checked through a separately trusted channel.

### Spending and uncertainty

I choose A, B and C at \$3,000. This repairs known access, guest-boundary and
recovery weaknesses. I defer D, retain basic logging, and have Mara assign
daily log review and an immediate staff reporting route. That leaves a
detection-delay risk which the director must review, not forget.

The separate arithmetic for A is:

- Before: \$50,000 × 0.10 = \$5,000 annual expected loss.
- After: \$50,000 × 0.04 = \$2,000.
- Reduction: \$3,000; less \$1,000 annual cost = \$2,000 estimated net benefit.

The break-even post-control rate is 0.08. At 0.09, the reduction is only
\$500 and the net financial benefit is negative \$500. At 0.02 it is
positive \$3,000. The choice therefore depends on an uncertain estimate.
I cannot add up claimed reductions for several overlapping controls as if
they were independent. Legal duties and unacceptable harm also constrain
the decision, even when expected monetary benefit is negative.

### Testing a boundary

The original rule 1 permits Guest to Booking database before rule 2 is
considered. It also admits Public to Booking database. Remove that broad
permission. A corrected teaching policy allows only the four legitimate
flows named above, then denies every other new connection.

Test all four permitted flows and at least these three denied flows:
Guest to Booking database using Database, Public to Booking database using
Database, and Public to Web using Database. Expected results are four
allows and three denies. A policy that blocks everything fails the
legitimate-service tests. Testing only a successful website visit would
miss the database exposure.

### Responding and recovering

Mara can escalate at 09:15 without waiting for an attribution verdict. The
provider preserves evidence while containing access, documenting any conflict
between preservation and stopping damage. A log digest helps detect later
change; the collector, time, storage location and handling record establish
context. A digest alone does not prove who created the original log.

The rehearsal completes 150 minutes after disruption:
10 minutes to restoration start, 120 minutes restoring, and 20 minutes
validating. That fits the four-hour RTO in this exercise. It is one test,
not a guarantee of future recovery.

With the original 23:00 recovery point, a 09:20 failure could lose
620 minutes of work, which fails the one-hour RPO. If option C supplies
a validated, unaffected 09:00 recovery point, the gap is 20 minutes.
For this comparison assume those recovery points are available and tested.
In a real incident, the newest backup might contain compromised data;
the provider must establish an acceptable recovery point before reconnecting.

Backups do not undo the export. Mara records what is known, preserves the
report, and asks the privacy adviser to assess coverage, information involved,
likely harm and remedial action. The adviser records the applicable
notification decision and timing. Neither a successful restore nor an
unconfirmed actor identity closes that assessment.

### Human process and review

Staff get a known reporting channel for unexpected prompts and requests.
A high-risk account reset or export approval uses a second, independent
verification route. Staff should not call a number supplied in the suspicious
message. The aim is quick reporting and constrained damage, without requiring
perfect suspicion from everyone.

The dossier ends with owners and evidence: Mara checks access tests and
reporting arrangements; the provider supplies boundary and recovery results;
the privacy adviser owns notification advice; the director reviews residual
risk and the deferred alert service. Revisit after a permissions change,
a failed restore test or a new service, as well as at the scheduled review.

## Independent transfer: weekend opening 

Work for 30–45 minutes without AI or the worked answer. All facts stay the
same except these changes:

- Budget is \$2,200. Option prices stay unchanged.
- The director requires option A before the new programme opens because
  bulk-export permissions are unacceptable. This is a supplied constraint.
- The RTO is now two hours; the RPO stays one hour.
- Restore takes 100 minutes, preceded by 10 minutes of response and
  followed by 20 minutes of validation.
- If C is selected, the latest validated recovery point is 09:00 for a
  09:20 disruption. Without C it is still 23:00 the previous day.
- The changed loss estimate is \$80,000, initial annual rate 0.10 and
  estimated rate after A of 0.06.

Write a one-page decision: assets, control choices and annual cost; one
permission and one boundary test; expected-loss arithmetic; RTO/RPO checks;
incident ownership and evidence; privacy escalation; and the unresolved
decision about opening. You may say “not ready”. Do not invent a free control,
a faster restore, an extra budget or proof that a breach did not happen.

### Check your reasoning 

A and C use the full \$2,200. A and B cost \$1,800 but leave the stated
backup problem. A and D cost \$2,000 but repair neither recovery nor the
network boundary. With A required, no affordable combination fixes all three.

For A, the changed expected loss is \$8,000 before and \$4,800 after;
the \$3,200 reduction gives \$2,200 net benefit after its cost.
The proposed recovery needs 130 minutes, so it misses the two-hour
RTO by 10 minutes, even if C meets the RPO at 20 minutes.
A+C leaves the network weakness unresolved. A defensible answer escalates
the launch decision, identifies the missing boundary repair and demands
a tested recovery improvement or an explicitly authorised change of target.
It does not quietly claim the service is ready.

Score one point for each of eight checks: correct assets and permissions;
affordable choices satisfying the A constraint; correct expected-loss maths;
both allow and deny tests; correct RTO and RPO findings; evidence-preserving
response with owners; a qualified privacy escalation; and an explicit
unresolved launch decision. Aim for at least seven, with no safety failure,
no claim that the 130-minute recovery meets a 120-minute target, and no
unsupported “ready” conclusion. A different control choice can pass if its
limitations and consequences are honestly defended.

# Further Reading 

This is a route into primary guidance, not a list to memorise. Consult the
source for the decision you are making. Standards, product behaviour and
legal requirements can change; these links were selected in September 2026.

## Foundations, identity and cryptography

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework organises
outcomes and governance. ASD's Essential Eight
provides a different resource: mitigation strategies and maturity guidance.
Neither removes the need to understand the organisation's risks.

NIST SP 800-63B-4 distinguishes
authenticators, password verifiers, recovery and sessions.
OWASP's password-storage guidance
explains implementation choices. Read these when assessing a login design,
rather than implementing a password store from a textbook sketch.

FIPS 186-5 covers digital signatures;
TLS 1.3 shows authentication,
key establishment and traffic protection as separate operations.
They are references for checking explanations, not prerequisites for the
paper exercises.

## Software, networks and incidents

The CVE programme,
CWE catalogue and
CERT/CC disclosure guide
help distinguish a vulnerability record, a recurring weakness and the
process of coordinating disclosure.

OWASP's access-control guidance
and XSS prevention guidance
expand the web chapter. Wireshark's Ethernet capture guidance
helps explain the limits of an observation point.

NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3
connects incident response to risk management.
CISA's ransomware guide
includes preparation, data theft and recovery.
NIST's contingency-planning resource
supports recovery objectives and testing.

## Legal decisions and historical evidence

OAIC's NDB guidance
and EDPB's GDPR notification guidance
describe different regimes. Coverage, facts, recipients and thresholds
matter. Use authorised advice for a real incident.

The Senate staff report on Target
is an analysis of reporting available in 2014. Treat its proposed
interventions as possible opportunities, not proven counterfactuals.
The chapter on cybercrime links the investigating agencies' accounts of
Gameover Zeus and Mirai.

The NCSC assessment of AI threats to 2027
is a dated assessment. Separate observed capability, forecast and your own
inference when using it.

checks for optional technical work. Incident Zero
offers group practice. Its materials have their own licence; this book
links them without incorporating them.
